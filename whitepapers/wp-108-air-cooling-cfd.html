Air cooling with ThermStack Flow: a JEDEC package in still air and in a wind tunnel

AbstractThermStack Flow solves the air flow and the heat in the solids together, so the cooling comes out of the simulation and is not an assumed heat-transfer coefficient. This paper applies it to the standard JEDEC test case: a 1 W die in a 15 mm package on a 2s2p test board, in the JESD51-2 still-air enclosure and in a JESD51-6 wind tunnel at 1 and 2 m/s. The junction-to-ambient resistance θJA is within 1.9 % of a commercial CFD solver in the wind tunnel and within 2.8 % of a commercial FEM solver in still air. After a 1 W power-on step, the die thermal impedance Zth(t) follows a commercial CFD transient within 3.2 % of the rise from 10 ms to 1,000 s. Cell counts, wall time and memory are given for every run.

1 / TEST CASE

The JEDEC package and its two air environments

One package, one board, two standard air environments, all from the JEDEC JESD51 family.

A 6 × 6 × 0.3 mm silicon die dissipates 1 W in a 15 mm package: a 1.2 mm mould cap over a 0.4 mm substrate and a 0.3 mm ball layer. The package sits at the centre of a 114.3 × 76.2 × 1.6 mm 2s2p test board (17.8 W/m·K in plane, 0.31 W/m·K through the board). In still air (JESD51-2) the board is held horizontally at mid height in a closed 305 mm cube with 300 K walls, and the air moves only by buoyancy. In the wind tunnel (JESD51-6) the same board faces a uniform 300 K stream along its long side. The air is laminar with Boussinesq buoyancy; radiation is off. Symmetry is used: a quarter model in still air and a half model in the tunnel.

2 / STILL AIR VS FORCED AIR

How the air carries the heat away

In still air the package heats the air above it, which rises as a plume and draws cooler air in along the board from both sides. The air moves at most 0.25 m/s. In the tunnel the stream sweeps the warm air off the board and carries it downstream. At 1 m/s the die rise drops from 40.0 K to 29.3 K. Doubling the speed to 2 m/s removes only 2 K more: 88–89 % of the heat leaves the package through the board, so the board’s own spreading and surface area set most of θJA.

3 / θJA AGAINST COMMERCIAL SOLVERS

Steady θJA within 3 % at every air speed

The same geometry, properties and boundary conditions were solved with a commercial CFD solver in the wind tunnel and with a commercial FEM solver with laminar natural convection in still air. θJA is the die peak rise per watt, so for this 1 W case its error in K/W is also the error in kelvin. In the tunnel, ThermStack Flow on a 201.6 k-cell grid is within 0.56 K (1.9 %) at 1 m/s and 0.05 K (0.18 %) at 2 m/s of the commercial CFD solver on its finest, 545 k-cell mesh. The commercial solver itself still moved by 0.77 K/W on its last mesh refinement; its extrapolated value, 29.1–29.6 K/W at 1 m/s, brackets the ThermStack Flow result. In still air the plume needs a finer grid: 2.8 % on 564 k cells, and 1.2 % after extrapolating the two ThermStack grids.

4 / POWER-ON TRANSIENT

Zth(t) from 1 ms to steady state, with the air flow evolving

At t = 0 the air is at rest and everything is at 300 K; then the die switches to 1 W. ThermStack Flow solves the flow and the heat together at every time step, so the plume builds up as the package warms. The step size grows from 1 ms to hundreds of seconds, 25 steps per decade, with second-order (BDF2) time integration. The commercial CFD solver ran the same still-air case as a transient in six chained segments, one per decade. ThermStack Flow follows the reference Zth(t) within 0.16 K up to 1 s and within 3.2 % of the rise at all times from 10 ms to 1,000 s (largest at 24 s). The largest absolute difference is 0.69 K, near 30 s. It has the size of the grid error of this 95.6 k-cell grid: the steady still-air rise moves by 0.65 K from this grid to the 564 k-cell grid.

5 / COST

Cells, wall time and memory

Every run used one CPU server. The ThermStack Flow wind-tunnel runs used a third of the commercial solver’s cells and half its memory, at a similar wall time. The commercial CFD solver ran its steady wind-tunnel cases on one core, so those wall times are not like for like. In the transient, ThermStack Flow iterates the coupled flow and energy at every one of its 66 steps and took longer than the commercial solver on 16 cores.
